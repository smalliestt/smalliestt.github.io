210° · Chit Chat

Chit Chat

Web platform with AI characters · Product design · Game economy · Startup, May–September 2023

Role
Product and economy designer, UX/UI
Timeline
May–September 2023
Platform
Responsive web
Scope
Onboarding, characters, chat, gacha, economy

Design notes and plans for interviews ↗

The chat interface

Chit Chat is a web platform for conversations with AI characters, with collecting and a shared story world built around the chat. The design challenge was to make these game systems feel exciting while keeping everyday conversation familiar.

I wanted the core interface to remain recognisable as the product grew. Telegram was a reference for that continuity: new features can arrive without making people relearn where conversations live. In Chit Chat, the chat list, navigation and message area form that stable foundation; new characters and events provide the changing content.

Characters and their stories

The characters belong to one world, connected by friendships, intrigues and investigations. People can follow those connections through questions or simply have an open conversation. This gives the chat both an immediate use and a longer thread to return to.

Story apps such as Romance Club were a reference for the appeal of getting to know characters over time. My direction brought that interest into a messenger: character profiles introduce interests in the character’s own voice, while suggested replies offer a starting point when someone does not know what to say. The intention was to make discovery lead naturally into a conversation.

My role

As product, economy and UX/UI designer, I worked across the first visit, sign-up, character library, profiles, conversations and gacha. I connected the interface flow with the currency model so that finding a character, earning gems and trying a banner belonged to the same product logic.

I proposed gacha as a defining part of the concept. Drawing on the character-collecting games that inspired the project at the time, I wanted meeting someone new to feel like a discovery. A subscription would not directly unlock the whole cast: characters would be obtained through pulls, using currency earned through tasks or purchased in the shop.

Drop rates and currency

I designed the probability and currency model alongside the screens: character drop rates, a guaranteed five-star after a set number of pulls, and soft pity that increases the odds before that guarantee. The banner exposes the odds and the remaining distance to the guarantee, so the next attempt has visible context.

The currency model connects tasks, daily rewards and gem packs to spending on pulls. The Bestie Pass offers a daily supply of gems rather than direct access to every character. Sign-up grants 50 gems, enough for five initial pulls, giving newcomers a way to try the collecting mechanic before buying currency.

Colour and navigation

I chose a blue-to-green gradient to give the product an energetic, recognisable accent. Most of the interface stays neutral, with dark chat panels and light content surfaces. Against that base, the gradient draws attention to messages, character announcements and the next action without making every element equally loud.

The persistent announcement at the top keeps a new character visible across the experience. On the left, chats and the compact task entry keep ongoing conversations and ways to earn currency close at hand. This supports two different intentions: returning to someone you know and looking for someone new.

Finding characters and buying gems

Character discovery has several entry points: the home banner, the library and the announcement strip. I also proposed a prompt to try for a character alongside the profile, on the right, when curiosity about that character is already high. The profile shown here demonstrates the character introduction and direct chat action; the extra pull prompt is an extension of that flow.

Purchase offers sit in the gem shop and gacha flow, where the user is already considering currency or a pull. Pack bonuses and the first-purchase offer make the options visible there, while tasks offer a way to earn gems. The aim was to support monetisation without letting purchase messages dominate the conversation.

What needs testing

The work connects a familiar messenger layout with character discovery, a shared narrative and an explicit game economy. My central hypothesis was that the anticipation of meeting a character, followed by an ongoing story, could make that relationship feel more personal.

The next validation would check whether newcomers understand how characters are obtained, can find a route to earned gems, and move from a first pull into a meaningful chat.

What’s in the design

Chats sit on the left of the home screen, next to the character library. On the gacha screen, the character, pull buttons and drop rates have separate areas.

What this could help with

Keeping chats in the same place could help people find their way back after browsing characters. The question is whether collecting adds to the conversation or starts to get in its way.

What to check next

Check whether someone can explain the cost and odds before a pull, then return to a chat without help. Look at the reveal animation separately: enjoying it does not necessarily mean someone wants to keep talking to the character.

Related reading
  1. Ryan & Deci · 2000 · Self-determination theory ↗Ryan and Deci discuss choice and connection as parts of motivation. For this project, that raises a practical question: can someone choose how to take part and leave when they want?
  2. Zendle & Cairns · 2018 · Loot boxes and problem gambling ↗This paper is a reason to ask about spending pressure and understanding the odds, alongside the appeal of collecting.

Random rewards can be exciting, but they can also feel frustrating or push people to keep spending. Zendle and Cairns found a link between loot-box spending and problem gambling; their survey does not tell us which causes which, or what would happen in Chit Chat.

Plans for interviews and group discussions

These sessions have not taken place yet. Individual interviews will cover the tasks below; group discussions can explore how people read the design.

  1. Find an existing conversation from the home screen.
  2. Explain one pull’s cost and randomness using the supplied screen; record any information the design does not communicate.
  3. Inspect a character, then return without making a purchase.

Note where people hesitate, what they think a pull costs, how they read the odds and how they get back to chat. Ask what interests them about the character without treating that answer as proof of a lasting connection.

eMeditation

Meditation app concept · UX/UI · Swift prototype · Personal project

Role
Product designer, UX/UI
Platform
iOS
Prototype
Swift
Scope
Research to prototype, on my own

Design notes and plans for interviews ↗

The app concept

eMeditation is a personal iOS app concept for short meditation and breathing practices. I wanted someone to open it, recognise a suitable activity and begin with very little setup. My starting point was the visual language of Ukrainian digital services, especially Diia: concise screens, clear blocks of information and familiar emoji.

I developed the project independently, from exploring scenarios and the visual system to a Swift prototype. The focus was the full practice journey: choosing an activity, setting it up, following it and seeing what was completed.

Three types of practice

The home screen offers a short timer, guided meditation and a two-minute breathing exercise. Each entry describes a concrete action, so someone can choose between practising alone, listening to guidance or following a breathing rhythm without first navigating a large catalogue.

I used three main accent colours—muted green, pale yellow and soft pink—to separate information into recognisable blocks. Familiar emoji give activities a quick visual cue. The subdued palette supports the calm tone I wanted for the app; dark text and generous type keep the content readable.

Setting the timer

Small numerals and cramped duration pickers were a personal frustration in the timer interfaces I considered. I wanted setting the time to be one of the easiest parts of the app, so I made the selected duration the main visual element.

Minutes and seconds sit in separate horizontal selectors, with the selected value much larger than its neighbours. The adjacent numbers explain the direction of adjustment, while a distinct Start button gives the setup a clear endpoint. Sound choices sit above the duration, keeping the primary decision in focus.

The breathing exercise

The breathing flow introduces the sequence before it starts: inhale for four counts, hold for seven, exhale for eight. During the exercise, each phase fills the screen with its own soft colour, a short instruction and a large count.

Green, pink and yellow distinguish the three stages. Colour reinforces the instruction rather than carrying it alone: the words and count remain visible. This keeps the screen simple during a practice, when reading a dense explanation would compete with following the rhythm.

After a session

Finishing a short practice deserves a response. For the completion flow, I proposed a brief congratulatory message that acknowledges the time the person made for themselves, followed by an optional mood check-in. This is encouragement without requiring a long reflection after every session.

The mood screen shown here uses five familiar expressions, from tense to calm, and an optional note. People can save a response or skip it. That lets the experience recognise how someone feels without turning the end of a practice into another task.

Practice history

The progress screen brings together a calendar, completed sessions, time spent practising and recent activities. These views answer different questions: when did I practise, how much time did I make for it, and what did I do?

I included progress even in this small app because a one-minute session can otherwise feel easy to overlook. Making those moments visible was intended to help people recognise their effort and return. The counts in the mockups are illustrative.

Onboarding, settings and offline use

I designed the surrounding states as part of the experience: onboarding, subscription, settings, mood check-in, practice history, offline access and the decision to leave an exercise. They explain what happens before and after the central action, rather than leaving those moments to generic dialogs.

The offline state points people to downloaded meditations. The exit dialog explains that leaving the breathing exercise will not count toward statistics. A separate support card proposes donating half of the revenue from an optional short ad to the Armed Forces of Ukraine.

What needs testing

The concept and Swift prototype establish the interaction direction. The next usability check would focus on whether people can set a duration without explanation, distinguish the breathing stages and find their previous sessions.

I would also check whether the completion message and statistics feel encouraging, especially after a break in practice. The intended effect is a calmer start and a clearer sense of progress; a change in habit or wellbeing would need separate evidence.

What’s in the design

The selected minutes and seconds are much larger than the numbers beside them. The progress screen shows a calendar, completed sessions and time spent practising. The totals shown in the design are examples.

What this could help with

The large numbers make the selected time easy to spot. Showing completed sessions could help someone notice the time they have made for themselves. A streak might feel less encouraging after a few missed days.

What to check next

Ask someone to set a specific duration and watch where they need to correct it. Then show the progress screen as if they were returning after a break, and ask which parts feel useful or discouraging.

Related reading
  1. Harkin et al. · 2016 · Monitoring goal progress ↗Harkin and colleagues studied whether monitoring progress helps people reach goals. This makes session history worth testing, including how it feels when someone has missed a few days.
  2. Elliot & Maier · 2014 · Colour psychology and its limits ↗Elliot and Maier discuss how colour effects depend on context. Here, the useful questions are whether the screens are comfortable to look at and easy to read.

Research on tracking progress gives a reason to explore this feedback; it does not show that this app builds a habit. The soft colours were chosen for the tone of the design. Whether people find them comfortable and readable still needs checking.

Plans for interviews and group discussions

These sessions have not taken place yet. Individual interviews will cover the tasks below; group discussions can explore how people read the design.

  1. Set the timer to eight minutes and thirty-five seconds, then point to Start.
  2. Identify what to do in each breathing phase using words and numbers as well as colour.
  3. Find a previous session and skip the optional mood check-in.

Record the time someone meant to set, the time they actually selected and any help they needed. Ask how they read the totals and what they would think on returning after a break. A short session cannot tell us whether the app changes a long-term habit.

Design notes and plans for interviews ↗

Why I made this series

World is a triptych I made at the start of Russia’s full-scale war against Ukraine in 2022. It reflects my response to the world’s reaction: realisation, a sense of betrayal and the wait for action. I wanted to give those feelings a visible form across three connected works.

This is a personal art series. The design decisions serve an emotional statement: the portraits, colour fragments and handwritten words work together to communicate the tension of that moment.

Photos, colour and handwriting

I built the compositions in Procreate using black-and-white stock photographs of people in traditional Ukrainian dress. The restrained photographs provide a common foundation; painted fragments, yellow outlines and handwriting interrupt it with colour and a more immediate, personal voice.

The repeated visual language holds the three panels together while allowing each composition to stand alone. Viewed as a group, the portraits and lettering become a continuous wall of images and statements. My role covered the concept, image selection, collage, colour treatment and handwritten elements.

Exhibition and award

World was shown at PRAY4UKRAINE, a charity exhibition that directs proceeds from sold works to the Armed Forces of Ukraine. The triptych also won a prize in the Fine Art category of the Nezalezhne Slovo (Independent Word) project. The award ceremony took place in Lviv on 1 June 2023.

The series shows how I use contrast, repetition and lettering to carry a message across several compositions. Presenting it outside the original digital format also brought the three panels together as a public statement.

What’s in the design

The reviewed panels use black-and-white portraits, yellow outlines and orange handwriting. Different coloured details interrupt each portrait, while the repeated treatment links the images.

What this could help with

Those shared details give viewers a way to connect the panels. The meaning they take from the portraits and writing may depend on what they already know about Ukrainian culture and the war.

What to check next

Show the works before giving the background. Ask what people notice and what connects the panels, then introduce the artist’s statement. Keep the different readings, including ones that do not match the original intention.

Related reading
  1. Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman · 2004 · Processing fluency and aesthetic pleasure ↗This paper looks at how features such as repetition and figure–ground contrast relate to aesthetic responses. It offers a way to think about the shared outlines and lettering, without prescribing how the work should feel.
  2. Wolfe & Horowitz · 2017 · Five factors that guide visual search ↗Wolfe and Horowitz explain that attention depends on meaning and previous experience as well as visual contrast. That is why the viewer’s context matters here.

These notes describe the artwork and possible ways of reading it. The papers cannot tell us what a particular viewer will feel or whether they will share the artist’s interpretation.

Plans for interviews and group discussions

These sessions have not taken place yet. Individual interviews will cover the tasks below; group discussions can explore how people read the design.

  1. View one panel and describe what is visible before interpreting it.
  2. View the three together and identify any links.
  3. Read the artist’s context statement, then explain what changed and what remained ambiguous.

Keep what people describe separate from what they think it means. Note how their reading changes after hearing the context. Agreement with the artist is not the goal of the interview.

Design notes and plans for interviews ↗

The previous menu

The starting point was the restaurant’s old menu, documented in photographs on its Google Maps listing. Across nine pages, dense black text, Polish and English descriptions, and prices against dashed lines gave many elements similar visual weight.

My redesign focused on the reading task at the table: find a category, scan the dishes and compare the relevant details. I wanted the menu to express the character of a Vietnamese restaurant while giving names, prices and dietary signs distinct roles.

The new page layout

I organised the redesign into eight pages, moving from starters and soups through main dishes to drinks and wine. Each category opens with a rounded red heading, paired with its Vietnamese name in serif italics. This gives guests a visible point to enter a page before reading individual dishes.

Rounded, informal type carries the Polish dish names, with Vietnamese names set beside them in serif italics. Prices form a separate red column on the right, creating a consistent place to look when comparing options. Spicy, vegetarian and gluten-free signs use a legend repeated at the foot of each page, so guests do not need to turn back for an explanation.

The dotted dragon

A dragon assembled from red dots runs through the inside pages as a continuous graphic motif. Its rhythm connects the menu to the wider red-and-cream identity, while the dish list stays the main reading surface. The balance changes with the amount of information on each page, while the type and price hierarchy stay consistent.

My role and the final files

My work covered the page structure, typography, colour hierarchy, dietary-sign system and placement of illustrations. The deliverable shown here is the eight-page menu design, supported by a comparison with the previous layout.

The redesign establishes a consistent place for each kind of information: expressive headings for orientation, a quieter dish list for reading, and a separate price column for comparison. A useful next check would be to observe how guests find a category and compare two dishes at the table.

Designing for KSI

Since November 2024, I have worked as a promotion ambassador and designer for KSI, the Computer Science Students’ Association at Jagiellonian University. I create event posters, graphics and T-shirt prints for a community that meets around both technical interests and social activities.

The KSI mark was the only fixed identity element at the start. My task was to build visual directions around it that could suit a machine learning workshop, a conference, a grill or a tournament. The challenge was to give each event its own character while keeping the association visible.

Posters for the university corridors

I designed the posters for a busy university corridor, where someone might only glance at the wall while rushing from one class to the next. Across the series, I chose bright, contrasting colours and large type to help each poster stand out in that brief moment. The hierarchy gives the event title a clear first read, followed by the details needed to take part.

I approached every poster with a distinct visual idea. The machine learning poster turns the word “model” into a spiral; the grill poster uses a halftone ξ. I wanted each new event to bring something fresh to the wall: a composition that catches attention on the way past and rewards a closer look. Making the posters visually appealing and interesting to encounter was part of the brief I set myself.

The concept and colour support a practical invitation: what is happening, when, where and how to take part. Every poster shown here was published. The originals are in Polish; I prepared the English versions for this portfolio.

The printed T-shirts

For merchandise, I drew on programming, binary code and symbols familiar to KSI members. The binary tee pairs a small ξ on the chest with a field of zeros and ones on the back. The cobalt tee combines a pixel outline with a Polish manifesto about the community.

Both designs went to print. The gallery separates the artwork from lookbook mockups, which show how the binary design could be presented in a wider visual context.

Other T-shirt concepts

The unprinted concepts explore other ways to represent the group: a tour tee built around the year’s events, a field guide to the species of the KSI ecosystem, three icons for compile, meet and inspire, and a ξ assembled from keyboard keys.

Each direction starts from something members can recognise—shared events, habits or tools—and translates it into a graphic idea. These are concept explorations, shown separately from the two produced designs.

Published and printed work

The published posters and two printed tees show how I adapt a limited starting identity to different messages and formats. My contribution spans visual concepts, typography, composition and artwork for posters and merchandise.

The outcome is a body of work used by the association, with a distinction between published posters, produced merchandise and exploratory concepts. Together, they show both the range of the visual direction and how it translates into finished pieces.

What’s in the design

The machine-learning poster uses bright blue, a large white title and words arranged in a circle. The cobalt T-shirt places a Polish text about the community over a pixel-outline symbol.

What this could help with

The colour and graphic idea may get someone to look. They still need to notice what the event is, when it happens and where to go. The circular lettering could help draw attention or compete with those details.

What to check next

Start with a brief view of a Polish original and ask what the person remembers. Then let them look again and find the date, room and sign-up details. For the shirts, compare what KSI members and newcomers make of the symbols.

Related reading
  1. Wolfe & Horowitz · 2017 · Five factors that guide visual search ↗This review explains why bright colour is only part of getting noticed: what someone is looking for and the setting around the poster also matter.
  2. Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman · 2004 · Processing fluency and aesthetic pleasure ↗The repeated symbols raise a question about familiarity. Do members recognise them quickly, and what do they communicate to someone outside KSI?

The posters were published and the shirts were printed. That tells us the work was used, but not how well people remembered it or whether it changed attendance. A corridor test should record the viewing distance and lighting.

Plans for interviews and group discussions

These sessions have not taken place yet. Individual interviews will cover the tasks below; group discussions can explore how people read the design.

  1. View a Polish original at a recorded distance for three seconds, hide it, then describe the event without prompts.
  2. View it again at normal reading distance and locate date, room and sign-up route.
  3. Compare an original and a simplified hierarchy version in counterbalanced order.

Keep unaided recall separate from finding details on a second look. Record distance, lighting and exposure time. Three seconds is a proposed test condition, not a claim about everyone’s attention span.
