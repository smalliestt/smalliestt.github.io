Chats and new characters

How easy is it to find a new character and then get back to a conversation?

A retrospective review of the finished design, coded in MAXQDA and ATLAS.ti. User interviews are planned and have not taken place yet.

Design choices to test

Keeping chats in the same place could help people find their way back after browsing characters. The question is whether collecting adds to the conversation or starts to get in its way.

Check whether someone can explain the cost and odds before a pull, then return to a chat without help. Look at the reveal animation separately: enjoying it does not necessarily mean someone wants to keep talking to the character.

Related reading

  1. Ryan & Deci · 2000 · Self-determination theory ↗Ryan and Deci discuss choice and connection as parts of motivation. For this project, that raises a practical question: can someone choose how to take part and leave when they want?
  2. Zendle & Cairns · 2018 · Loot boxes and problem gambling ↗This paper is a reason to ask about spending pressure and understanding the odds, alongside the appeal of collecting.

Random rewards can be exciting, but they can also feel frustrating or push people to keep spending. Zendle and Cairns found a link between loot-box spending and problem gambling; their survey does not tell us which causes which, or what would happen in Chit Chat.

Interviews to run next

Who to speak to

Adults who use chat or character-story products; include people who avoid paid random rewards. Do not select only gacha enthusiasts.

Questions for individual interviews · IDI

  1. Tell me about the last time you returned to a character-based story. What brought you back?
  2. Without pulling yet, what do you think this screen offers and what would it cost?
  3. Where would you go if you wanted to continue an existing conversation?
  4. What would make you stop or leave this interaction?

Tasks to try

  1. Find an existing conversation from the home screen.
  2. Explain one pull’s cost and randomness using the supplied screen; record any information the design does not communicate.
  3. Inspect a character, then return without making a purchase.

Note where people hesitate, what they think a pull costs, how they read the odds and how they get back to chat. Ask what interests them about the character without treating that answer as proof of a lasting connection.

Topics for a group discussion · FGI

Optional: two separate groups of 4–6 adults, one familiar with character collection and one less familiar. Compare interpretations of profiles and story cues after private written reactions. Use IDI for spending and pressure; a group cannot establish individual purchase comprehension.

About this review

The review uses the full case-study text, the design files shown in the portfolio and the published research listed above. Each coded fragment keeps the observation separate from its interpretation. The notes describe the design and what to test next; they do not report how real users responded.
